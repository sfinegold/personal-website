How do you move a cursor with a thought?

Neuralink puts about a thousand hair-thin wires into the part of the brain that plans movement. Those wires don't hear the word left. They hear crackling. This page is how the crackling becomes a cursor — and four parts of it you can play with yourself. No background needed.

Off by default. Every idea here is written out in ordinary words. The notation is an optional second copy for anyone who wants it.
A

Steer a dot you're not allowed to touch

Move your pointer around inside the black box below. You are not moving the blue dot. You never touch the blue dot.

What your pointer does is set an intention — a direction you'd like to go. That intention is handed to 128 pretend brain cells, and the only thing they do in response is crackle. A program listens to the crackling, tries to work out which way you meant, and nudges the blue dot that way. Your job is to get it into the orange rings.

This is the real situation for someone who is paralyzed. The intention is completely intact. What's broken is the wiring between the intention and the hand. Everything on this page is about rebuilding that wiring out of software.

B
The signal

Imagine a stadium crowd where everyone is facing a slightly different way.

No one person tells you the answer. The average of the crowd does.

One brain cell is a vote, not an answer

Picture a crowd where each person faces a slightly different direction. Ask the crowd to move right. The people already facing right cheer loudest. The ones facing up-and-right cheer a bit. The ones facing left go quiet. Nobody announces "right" — but if you look at where the loud people are facing and average it, you get right.

That is close to literally how this part of the brain works. Each cell has a favourite direction. It fires fastest for that direction and tapers off smoothly as you move away from it. Nothing is a readout. Everything is a vote.

The rule, in one sentence
How fast a cell fires = its resting rate, plus how much it likes the direction you chose.
Cosine tuning · Georgopoulos et al., 1982 λi(v) = b0i + mi (ui · v) = b0i + mi ‖v‖ cos(θ − θi)

That relationship is simple enough to run backwards, which is the whole trick. If you know each cell's favourite direction, and you can hear how hard each one is firing, you can work back to the direction that would have produced this pattern of shouting.

Two things make it harder than a crowd:

  • Cells are unreliable witnesses. Ask the same cell the same question twice and it answers differently, because the timing of its firing is partly random. It's right on average and untrustworthy on any single occasion.
  • You're only hearing a few of them. A thousand wires in a region containing millions of cells. You aren't reading the crowd — you're polling a small, arbitrary sample of it.

Both problems have the same answer: listen to more cells, and weight each one by how much you trust it. That's why the cell-count slider in panel A moves the accuracy number so much.

There's a small bonus hidden in the unreliability. The randomness in these cells has a known shape — the busier a cell is, the more its count wobbles, and the two rise together in a fixed way. So the noise itself tells you how much to trust each cell on each occasion. It's a calibration you get for free, and a decoder that ignores it is throwing away something it was handed for nothing.

C
The trick

A recording of an orchestra is one wiggly line. Yet you can still pick out the violin.

This is how a machine does the same thing.

How do you hear one instrument in a recording?

Before anything can guess intentions, it has to find the crackles at all. And the raw recording from a single wire is a mess — like one microphone in a room with an orchestra playing, a fridge humming, and someone tapping a pencil. All of it arrives as a single wiggly line.

There's a trick for pulling that apart, and it is far simpler than its reputation.

Take your recording. Take a pure test wave — one clean note at one speed. Multiply the two together point by point, and average the result.

  • If your recording contains that note, the two rise and fall together. The multiplication keeps landing on positive, and the average comes out large.
  • If it doesn't, the two drift in and out of step. The multiplication is negative as often as positive, and the average cancels itself down to nothing.

That's the whole idea. Do it for every possible note, and you end up with a list of what's in the recording. That list is called a spectrum, and the procedure has a name: the Fourier transform.

Why two test waves and not one? Set the dial to 5 Hz and watch. Wave A scores 0.000 — as though there were no 5 Hz note in the recording at all. There obviously is; it's just a quarter-step out of sync, so peaks land on troughs and everything cancels. Wave B is the same wave shifted over, and it catches it perfectly at 1.000. One test wave can be fooled by timing. Two never are.

Now try 8 or 17 Hz, speeds that genuinely aren't in the recording. Both scores read zero. Not nearly zero — zero. Waves at different speeds cancel each other out exactly, which is why this method never smears one note into its neighbours.

The two test waves are the cosine and sine terms. The integral is the averaging you just watched, nothing more exotic.

One last thing, because it's the reason anyone can afford to do this. Done the obvious way, checking every note in a short recording takes about 17 million multiplications. A shortcut published in the 1960s — the Fast Fourier Transform — gets the identical answer in about 49,000. That saving of roughly 340× is the only reason your phone can do this while you're still talking into it.

D
The bottleneck

Spikes, brain waves and the building's mains hum all sit at different speeds.

That separation is what lets you keep one and bin the rest.

Three things are on the wire, and only one is useful

Point that trick at a real electrode and the mess sorts itself out. There are three sources, and they occupy different speeds:

  • Spikes. A brain cell firing makes a blip about a thousandth of a second long. Short blip means fast wiggle — these land around 1,000 wiggles per second.
  • Brain waves. The murmur of thousands of nearby cells, rising and falling over tens of milliseconds. Much slower, and much louder — several times the size of the spikes.
  • Mains hum. The building's electrical wiring leaks in at exactly 60 wiggles per second, everywhere in North America.

Turn off brain waves and watch spike loudness barely budge — 7.68 to 7.91. Turn off spikes and it falls off a cliff, to 1.27, which is just the electronics' own hiss. That gap is what makes the whole thing possible.

Because the three sit at different speeds, you can keep one and discard the others. That's what "filter to 300–6,000 Hz" means, and those numbers aren't arbitrary — they are the width of a spike, converted into a speed. Short things are fast. That's the only physics involved.

Now the awkward part: none of this fits down the wire

The implant listens to 1,024 wires, about 19,300 times a second each. Written out, that's roughly 200 million bits every second.

The implant is sealed inside a skull and talks to the outside world over Bluetooth, which carries maybe 1 million bits a second — and can't carry more without drawing power the implant doesn't have and making heat the surrounding tissue can't tolerate.

There is no clever compression that squeezes 200× out of something that's mostly noise. So the implant does something blunter. It doesn't send the recording at all. It listens on the spot, notices each spike as it happens, and sends only a tally: how many spikes on each wire in the last twentieth of a second. A thousand small numbers, fifty times a second. That fits comfortably.

The recording is destroyed inside your head, on purpose, and never leaves. What the software is allowed to see is decided by the hardware.

Which is why the job posting says the work will "inform our hardware roadmap." How many wires, how fast they sample, and what the chip measures before transmitting are not settled facts the software has to live with. They're open questions, and the person building the models is the person best placed to answer them.

A shortcut worth knowing
Measuring how much spike-sized activity is on a wire sounds like it needs the full note-by-note breakdown. It doesn't. A 200-year-old result says you get the identical answer by filtering the signal and measuring how loud it is — which is a few lines of arithmetic.
Parseval's theorem Σn |xn|2 = 1N Σk |Xk|2

That's why the two "spike loudness" numbers in the widget agree to three decimal places — they aren't two estimates of the same thing, they are the same number, arrived at from opposite directions. So the chip inside the head never does the note-by-note breakdown at all. The frequency view tells you what to measure; you then measure it the cheap way.

E
The guess

Three methods, forty years apart. All three are switchable in panel A.

Watch the shape of the movement change, not just the score.

Turning a thousand tallies back into a direction

So now you have a thousand numbers, fifty times a second, and you need exactly two out of them: how fast left-or-right, and how fast up-or-down.

1 — Everyone votes. Give each cell an arrow pointing its favourite way, make the arrow as long as the cell fired hard, and add them all up. It's a handful of lines of code and it genuinely works. Its flaw is that it assumes the cells you happen to be listening to are spread evenly around the compass. They never are — you got whichever cells the wires landed near — so the answer leans toward wherever your cells happen to be crowded.

2 — Best fit. Instead of assuming the spread is even, measure it during setup and correct for it. It's the same idea as drawing a best-fit line through a scatter plot, just done in two directions at once. Still fast enough to run in millionths of a second, and it removes the lean.

3 — Best fit, with memory. The first two treat every twentieth of a second as a fresh, isolated guess, which is exactly why their output twitches. But a real hand doesn't teleport. Where it was a moment ago tells you a great deal about where it is now.

What "with memory" actually does, each round
Predict where you probably are, from a moment ago. Look at what the cells just said. Then blend the two — leaning on whichever one you currently have more reason to trust.

That balance is the important part. When the cells are reporting clearly it leans on them. When they go quiet, or half the wires stop working, it leans on the prediction and coasts through. This is precisely why option 3 survives the broken-wires slider in panel A while the other two fall apart — it has something to fall back on.

A practical wrinkle. Written the textbook way, the memory method needs you to invert a 1,024-by-1,024 grid of numbers every twentieth of a second — hopelessly slow. Rearranged, the identical answer needs only a 2-by-2. Same result, roughly ten thousand times less work. A great deal of real-time engineering turns out to be finding the rearrangement.

Past these three, the field moves on to neural networks that learn the pattern instead of assuming it. The method with memory stays as the honest yardstick: if a large model can't beat it on a day it has never seen, it hasn't learned anything about brains. It has memorised one day's electrodes.

F
The person

Normally the thing you're learning from sits still. Here it's a person, watching, adjusting.

The person is learning too, which breaks the usual rules

Setting up a decoder starts out ordinary. The cursor moves on its own, the user imagines following it, and you collect matched pairs — brain did this, cursor did that. Standard stuff.

Then you hand over control, and the answers quietly go wrong.

Say the cursor is drifting to the left of the target. What is the user thinking at that moment? Not "left." They're thinking "right, hard" — they're correcting. So if you keep training on where the cursor actually went, you are patiently teaching the decoder to reproduce its own mistakes.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple. Assume that whatever the cursor did, the user was trying to go toward the target. Repoint every training example at the target, keep how fast it was moving, and retrain. One assumption, and control improves in a way that no amount of offline testing would have predicted.

The whole fix
Don't learn from where the cursor went. Learn from where the person obviously meant it to go.

There's a second problem, and it never goes away. The brain is not a fixed instrument. Electrodes shift by fractions of a millimetre, tissue reacts to them, threads pull loose, and attention and tiredness change the answers within a single afternoon. A decoder tuned on Monday is measurably worse by Friday.

So retuning can't mean a trip to the clinic. It has to happen constantly, in the background, working out what the person meant from context they're already producing — without them ever being asked to stop and recalibrate.

Every number you can measure in a lab is a stand-in. The real test is a person at home at 11pm trying to answer a text message.

G
The clock

Past about a tenth of a second, a cursor stops feeling like part of you.

Uneven delay is worse than long delay.

All of it has to happen in less than a blink

Window 20 ms — waiting to collect enough spikes Detect ~1 ms Bluetooth 8–15 ms Guess <1 ms Screen 8–16 ms

Notice where the time actually goes. The guessing — the part that sounds hardest — is nearly free. The two real costs are waiting to collect enough crackling to be confident, and the Bluetooth hop out of the head.

That waiting is a genuine dilemma, and you can feel it yourself. In panel A, drag the listening window down to 10 ms: the dot responds faster and gets twitchier, because it's deciding on half as much evidence. Drag it up to 100 ms: much smoother and steadier, but now it visibly trails your hand. There is no setting that wins both. Picking where to sit on that trade-off is one of the most consequential decisions in the entire system.

One subtlety: unevenness is worse than delay. A steady lag is something a person learns to lead by, the way you throw a ball slightly ahead of someone running. A lag that jumps around can't be learned at all. Which is why this software is written with unusual care about when things happen and not just what they compute — and why the posting asks for C, C++ or Rust, languages that let you control timing exactly.

How fast is fast enough?

There's a standard way to score pointing that lets you compare a brain implant to a trackpad on the same scale: how far away the target was, how small it was, and how long it took. It comes out in bits per second, and it's the number running live in panel A.

The posting asks for typing at 35 words a minute. If you treat every letter as a fresh surprise, that works out to about 13.7 bits per second — well beyond what any cursor delivers today.

But English isn't a string of surprises. After "the cat sat on the ma—" you can guess what's coming. Measured properly, English carries only about 1 bit per letter, so the amount that genuinely has to come out of the brain is closer to 3 bits per second. That's reachable.

The gap between 13.7 and 3 is a language model. Half of a typing implant is neuroscience. The other half is software that knows what a sentence looks like.

Two things make this unlike most machine learning work. The data keeps changing shape, because the sensor physically moves inside living tissue. And there is almost none of it — a handful of participants, each session costing real hours of a real person's day.

The field's usual answer to a hard problem is to throw more data at it. Here that option isn't available, which pushes everything toward strong assumptions, borrowing what you learned from one person to help the next, and simulations you can actually trust.
