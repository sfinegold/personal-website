What I tried

  • A campaign is ~5 functions — I lived in Data and Field.

What worked · 1

Poll analysis → ad targeting

  • AI found which voters were most persuadable by the candidate's message.
  • It structured the data, wrote the model code, set parameters, and ran cross-validation to pick the best fit — a slow specialist task compressed into days.
  • The catch: the binding input is a good poll — expensive and slow — and how you structure the data decides whether you can model movement.

What I'd do differently

The hard-won lessons

  • Earn internal trust + nail reliability at key moments. Field software cannot crap out on a GOTV weekend — that's how you lose the team.
  • Design data to model movement from day one. Retrofitting polling data is painful.
What I'd double down on

The campaign AI stack

  • Managed Claude + a curated campaign Google Drive — permission-aware, so people only get answers from what they're cleared to see.
  • Cheap mini-apps: endorsement tracker · questionnaire answers in the candidate's voice (human-in-the-loop) · strategic calendar — built on NGP (read/write the voter file).
  • Relational organizing (volunteers bring friends); AI for message consistency; AI calling for GOTV (others' win — my next experiment).
Built on GitHub · Vercel · Supabase.
Open questions

What I'm still chewing on

  • Ad & content development — we cracked ad targeting; using AI to help make the creative is still open.
  • Measuring voter response beyond polls — good polls are slow and expensive. What are faster, cheaper signals that a message is landing?
For the room

Considerations & the bet

  • Put controls + budget around AI — spend caps, governance, who-can-access-what.
  • Reset expectations of staff output — when one person can build and analyze this much, it changes scope and hiring.

For '26/'28 the edge compounds: cheaper analysis, cheaper software, a consistent message, deeper volunteer relationships.
